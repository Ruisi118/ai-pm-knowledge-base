# Source Filtering Guide

How to identify high-quality learning sources by domain type. Read this during Phase 1 of the rapid learning workflow.

---

## Why Source Filtering Matters

The original 48-hour learning method story hides a critical detail: the researcher already knew WHICH 6 textbooks and 15 papers to use. Source selection is itself an expert ability. This guide helps approximate that ability through systematic cross-verification.

---

## Evaluation Criteria by Domain Type

### STEM Fields (Physics, Math, Biology, Chemistry, CS)

**What to look for:**
- Textbooks used in university courses at top programs (MIT OCW, Stanford, Caltech syllabi are publicly available)
- Books that have survived multiple editions (longevity = community validation)
- Review/survey papers with high citation counts in the field's top journals
- Textbooks recommended on community wikis (e.g., Math Stack Exchange canonical book lists)

**Red flags:**
- "Made easy" or "for dummies" as sole source (fine as supplement, not foundation)
- Self-published textbooks without peer review
- Outdated editions when the field has evolved significantly

**Entry point strategy:** Start with a structured course (MIT OCW, Coursera from a top university) before touching textbooks. The lecture structure provides scaffolding.

### Social Sciences & Humanities (Economics, Psychology, Philosophy, History)

**What to look for:**
- Canonical works recognized across competing schools of thought
- Meta-analyses and systematic reviews (especially in psychology, sociology)
- Norton Anthologies or Cambridge Companions for humanities
- Textbooks that present multiple theoretical perspectives fairly

**Red flags:**
- Sources from only one theoretical camp (need both sides of debates)
- Pop-science books as primary sources (good supplements, not foundations)
- Anything that claims "the definitive answer" in inherently contested fields

**Entry point strategy:** Start with a survey course or handbook that maps the landscape of perspectives before diving into any single school of thought.

### Technical & Applied Fields (Engineering, Medicine, Law, Finance)

**What to look for:**
- Practitioner-respected references, not just academic ones
- Standards documents and professional body publications
- Case-study compilations from respected institutions
- Textbooks used in professional certification programs

**Red flags:**
- Purely theoretical sources without practical grounding
- Sources that are correct but outdated on current practice
- Blog posts or tutorials without institutional backing (for foundational learning)

**Entry point strategy:** Start with a professional overview or certification study guide to understand the landscape, then dive into specific technical references.

### Emerging Fields (AI/ML, Blockchain, Quantum Computing, Biotech)

**What to look for:**
- Preprint servers (arXiv) for cutting-edge work, but cross-reference with peer-reviewed versions
- Conference proceedings from top venues (NeurIPS, ICML for ML; IEEE for computing)
- Survey papers that map the current state of the field
- Expert blogs and technical reports from leading research labs

**Red flags:**
- Hype-driven content (especially in blockchain, AI)
- Sources more than 2-3 years old in fast-moving fields
- Marketing-disguised-as-education from vendors

**Entry point strategy:** Start with the most recent survey paper from a top venue. These are written to orient newcomers and map the landscape.

## Cross-Verification Technique

The most reliable heuristic when you can't evaluate sources directly:

1. **Ask from multiple angles**: "Top 5 textbooks in X" from academic, practitioner, and beginner-friendly perspectives
2. **Look for overlap**: Sources that appear across multiple recommendation lists are high-confidence picks
3. **Check recency vs. staying power**: For foundational topics, prefer classics. For applied topics, prefer recent + well-reviewed
4. **Verify with syllabi**: Check if the source appears in course reading lists from 2-3 top university programs
5. **Community consensus**: Check Reddit, Stack Exchange, or domain-specific forums for "what should I read first" threads—look for repeatedly mentioned titles

**The overlap principle**: If the same book appears in a university syllabus, a practitioner recommendation list, and a community FAQ, it's almost certainly a strong choice.

## Common Traps in Source Selection

| Trap | What Happens | How to Avoid |
|------|-------------|--------------|
| **Recency bias** | Assuming newest = best | Check if fundamentals have actually changed |
| **Popularity bias** | Most-read ≠ most accurate | Cross-check with expert recommendations, not bestseller lists |
| **Authority bias** | Famous author ≠ best treatment of every subtopic | A Nobel laureate's pop-science book may be worse than a textbook for learning |
| **Difficulty bias** | Harder ≠ better for learning | Match source difficulty to your current level |
| **Completeness bias** | Trying to find "the one book" | No single source covers everything well; 3-5 complementary sources > 1 comprehensive one |

## Teaching the Meta-Skill

When presenting sources to the user, always explain:
- **Why** you selected these specific sources (what signals you used)
- **How** the user can evaluate sources themselves in the future
- **What** you're less confident about (be honest about limits of AI source evaluation)

The goal is not just to give good sources—it's to help the user develop source evaluation as a transferable skill.
